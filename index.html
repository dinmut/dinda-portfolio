Case study: Customer Segmentation Using RFM Analysis and K-Means

Business problem

Segment customers by Recency, Frequency and Monetary value to target them more efficiently.

Dataset

Online retail transactions with invoice, customer, quantity, price and date fields.

Data preparation

Removed duplicates and rows with missing values, built R, F and M features per customer, removed outliers, and standardized the features.

Exploratory analysis

Boxplots to inspect outliers in Recency, Frequency and Monetary.

Key findings

Cluster 1 customers spent more and purchased more often than Cluster 0.

Recommendations

Run retention efforts for Cluster 0, personalize offers for Cluster 1, and use the segments for sharper targeting.

Limitations

Segmentation uses only three behavioural features, and silhouette analysis favoured just two clusters, which gives a coarse view of customers. Monetary values include negatives (likely returns or cancellations), and removing outliers may also remove some of the highest-value customers. The segments were not validated against later purchase behaviour.

Case study: Classifying Expert Diagnoses from Reported Mental Health Symptoms

Business problem

Understand which symptoms relate to an expert diagnosis and test whether a model can classify it.

Dataset

120 rows, 19 columns. All columns were text; no missing values or duplicates were found.

Data preparation

Dropped Patient Number, fixed the "Optimisim" column name and a stray "YES " value, grouped 1-9 ratings into Weak, Medium and Strong, then encoded to ordinal numbers.

Exploratory analysis

Distribution plots for symptom columns such as sleep disorder, exhaustion, euphoria and sadness.

Key findings

Sadness, exhaustion, sleep disorder, suicidal thoughts, nervous breakdown and overthinking correlated positively with the diagnosis.

Recommendations

With only 120 records and modest accuracy, the notebook is best treated as an exploratory study. Recommended next steps: collect a larger and more balanced sample, validate the model on separate data, and use the strongest correlated symptoms (such as sadness, exhaustion and suicidal thoughts) only as supporting signals for professional screening, never as a replacement for diagnosis.

Limitations

Accuracy stayed around 0.51 to 0.54 across models, and the dataset is small (120 rows). Results are not suitable for clinical use.

Case study: Predicting Health Insurance Charges from Customer Profiles

Business problem

Identify what drives insurance charges and predict them from customer attributes.

Dataset

1,338 rows, 7 columns, with one duplicate row removed and no missing values.

Data preparation

Outlier handling with the IQR method, one-hot encoding for sex and region, label encoding for smoker, and standardization.

Exploratory analysis

Charges rose with age, smokers were an imbalanced group, and charges contained many outliers.

Key findings

Age and smoker status had the highest correlation with charges. Linear Regression led on mean cross-validation score (about 0.60).

Recommendations

Consider pricing and customer segmentation by age and smoker status.

Limitations

The best model reached a mean cross-validation score of about 0.60, so a large share of the variation in charges is left unexplained. Outlier handling with the IQR method removes some very high charges, and the dataset has only 1,338 rows and no medical history. Findings describe association, not cause.

Case study: Predictive Maintenance: Predicting Machine Failures from Sensor Data

Business problem

Unplanned machine failures stop production, so the goal is to flag at-risk machines early enough to plan maintenance.

Dataset

AI4I 2020 Predictive Maintenance dataset (UCI), synthetic, 10,000 records with no missing values or duplicates. Failures make up 3.39% of records.

Data preparation

Failure-mode columns excluded from features to avoid leakage; engineered temperature difference, power and tool-wear-torque features; stratified 80/20 split with preprocessing inside a pipeline.

Exploratory analysis

Failure rate, failure rate by product type, records per failure mode (heat dissipation was the most frequent), feature distributions, and correlations.

Key findings

Gradient Boosting led with a cross-validated PR-AUC of 0.91 and a test PR-AUC of 0.90. At threshold 0.5 it raised 54 alerts with precision 1.00 and recall 0.79. Recall was 1.00 for power and overstrain failures and 0.90 for heat dissipation, but only 0.10 for tool wear failures. Temperature difference, rotational speed and power were among the most influential features.

Recommendations

A threshold near 0.3 balanced coverage and false alarms on the test set (precision 0.95, recall 0.85). Monitor temperature difference, rotational speed and power as early indicators, and handle tool wear separately because the model rarely catches it.

Limitations

The data is synthetic, so real factory data will be noisier. Thresholds were compared on the test set and should be validated on separate data. Per-mode recall rests on few cases (for example 10 tool wear and 4 random failures). Costs of missed failures and false alarms are not modelled.

Case study: Retail Sales Analysis: Profitability Drivers and Loss-Making Orders

Business problem

Strong sales do not guarantee profit, so the goal is to find where profit is made and lost and how discounts relate to it.

Dataset

Sample Superstore dataset, 9,994 rows and 21 columns of fictional US retail orders from 2014 to 2017, with no missing values or duplicates.

Data preparation

Parsed order and ship dates and created shipping time, month, profit margin and a loss-making flag. The train/test split was grouped by Order ID to avoid leakage.

Exploratory analysis

Profit and margin by category, sub-category, region and segment; margin and loss rate by discount band; monthly sales trend.

Key findings

Sales were about 2.30 million with a 12.5% profit margin, and 18.7% of order lines lost money. Furniture earned a 2.5% margin against about 17% for Technology and Office Supplies, and Supplies, Bookcases and Tables lost money overall. The Central region had the lowest margin (7.9%). Margin turned negative above a 20% discount: about 92% of lines at 20-50% discount and every line above 50% lost money. Holt-Winters beat the seasonal naive baseline on the last 6 months (MAPE 17.1% vs 25.4%), and Gradient Boosting flagged loss-making lines with a PR-AUC of about 0.95, driven mainly by discount.

Recommendations

Review discount rules above 20%, review pricing and discounting for Furniture and the loss-making sub-categories, investigate the Central region, and use the model to flag high-discount order lines before approval.

Limitations

The data is fictional and covers a limited window, so findings are illustrative. The forecast was checked on only 6 months. Correlation between discount and loss does not prove causation.
